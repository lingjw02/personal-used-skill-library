# Trademark screening

This is a search aid, never a legal opinion. An AI assistant cannot certify that a name or mark is legally available -- only a qualified trademark professional working through the appropriate jurisdictions can do that. Hold this line even if the user pushes back or seems to want reassurance.

## The three levels

**`none` (default when unspecified)**
Do no legal screening at all. Design the mark on its creative merits. If the user hasn't asked about trademarks, don't volunteer legal-sounding claims either way.

**`preliminary`**
- Search whatever official databases and web sources are actually accessible to you.
- Look for identical names and confusingly similar names/marks in the relevant category.
- Where supported, consider visually similar marks, not just name matches.
- Note the goods/services categories and jurisdictions this reasoning applies to.
- Present findings as **preliminary and non-exhaustive** -- explicitly, every time. Never say or imply "cleared," "safe to use," or "no conflicts" as a conclusion; say instead something like "no obvious conflict turned up in what I could search, but this isn't a legal clearance."
- If you don't have access to the databases needed to search properly, say so directly and offer a search checklist instead of guessing.

**`counsel_ready`**
Do the preliminary work above, then assemble a package a trademark attorney could actually use:
- Candidate name (exact spelling)
- Logo representation
- Goods/services description
- Intended jurisdictions
- Any potentially similar marks discovered during the preliminary pass
- The search terms and databases/sources actually reviewed
- Open questions the search couldn't resolve

## Why this matters

Public trademark search tools are explicitly scoped, and their own operators say so. The World Intellectual Property Organization's Global Brand Database supports searching by name, goods/services, and image similarity across participating collections, but WIPO itself notes that national or regional registers may also need to be searched separately, and that a trademark attorney may be the right next step. The USPTO similarly recommends a clearance search before filing because similarity to an existing mark and likelihood of confusion are common grounds for rejection -- and says a comprehensive U.S. search goes beyond any single federal database, typically also covering state registers and general web presence.

In short: a single database check is a starting point, not a clearance process. Reflect that scope honestly in whatever you tell the user.

## Language rules (apply at every screening level)

- Never say "trademark safe," "legally cleared," "no risk," or any equivalent -- regardless of what the search turned up.
- Always attribute findings to what was actually searched ("I checked X and found...") rather than implying a comprehensive legal review occurred.
- If a name/mark looks similar to something well-known, say so plainly rather than downplaying it to keep the user happy with their pick.
- Disclose jurisdictional scope -- a clean U.S. federal search says nothing about EU, UK, or other national registers unless those were actually checked too.
